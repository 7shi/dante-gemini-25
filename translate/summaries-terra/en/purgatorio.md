## Canto 1

Having left the cruel sea of Hell, the poet raises the little vessel of his intellect to sing of the second realm, where the human spirit is purified and becomes worthy to ascend to Heaven. He invokes the Muses and especially Calliope, asking that poetry rise again and that she aid him with the song that dealt the Pieres such a blow that they despaired of forgiveness. Having emerged from the dead air, he again delights in the serene sapphire-colored sky and the star of love that lights the east. Turning south, he sees four stars never seen except by the first people and laments that the north is deprived of them; then he turns his gaze toward the other pole, where the Chariot has already disappeared.

Dante sees beside him a solitary old man of an appearance so venerable that no father deserves as much reverence from a son: he is Cato, illuminated by the four holy stars. Cato asks the two who they are, how they could have fled the eternal prison by ascending against the infernal river, and whether the laws of the abyss or the heavenly will have changed. Virgil prompts Dante to show him respect and explains that a woman descended from Heaven sent him to aid the poet, still alive but close to death because of his own folly, and to guide him through Hell as far as the souls who purify themselves under Cato's guardianship. He then asks him to let them pass through his seven realms, since Dante seeks freedom, precious to Cato himself, who chose death for it at Utica, and he also invokes the love of Marcia, his wife dwelling in Limbo.

Cato replies that Marcia was dear to him in life and that he then did everything she asked of him, but now, dwelling beyond the infernal river, she can no longer move him because of the law established after his departure from Limbo. Since, however, a lady from Heaven guides Virgil and Dante, no further entreaties are needed: Cato allows them to proceed and orders Virgil to gird Dante with a plain reed and wash every trace of filth from his face, so that he may appear before the first minister of Paradise with no mist upon his eyes. He points out the reeds growing in the soft mud along the shore of the little island and, forbidding them to return by that way, says that the now-rising sun will show the easier path for climbing the mountain.

After Cato disappears, Dante silently follows Virgil, who leads them back along the lonely plain toward the shore, while dawn makes the shimmering of the sea recognizable. Having reached the place where dew resists the sun thanks to the shade, Virgil lays his hands on the wet grass and with it washes from Dante's tearful cheeks the color that Hell had hidden from him. On the deserted shore, Virgil girds Dante with the humble plant chosen by Cato, and another immediately grows again where he plucks it.

## Canto 2

At dawn, on the shore of Purgatory, Dante and Virgil see a light approaching over the sea with incomparable speed. They recognize an angel of God, who, without oars or sails, guides with his eternal wings a light boat carrying more than a hundred spirits. The spirits sing together “In exitu Israël de Aegypto,” then the angel blesses them with the sign of the cross, leaves them on the shore, and departs swiftly.

The souls who have just arrived, lost and bewildered in the new place, ask Dante and Virgil to show them the way to climb the mountain. Virgil replies that they too are inexperienced pilgrims, having arrived shortly before by another road so harsh that climbing will now seem like a game. Realizing from his breathing that Dante is still alive, the souls grow pale with wonder and crowd around to stare at him, almost forgetting to set out to purify themselves.

Dante recognizes in the soul whom he vainly tries to embrace his friend Casella, and asks him why he has taken so long to reach Purgatory. Casella explains that the heavenly boatman, whose will is just, had repeatedly denied him passage, but for three months has kindly received at the mouth of the Tiber all souls who wish to enter and are not destined for Acheron. Dante then asks him to comfort him by singing as he once did, and Casella sweetly intones “Love that speaks to me within my mind,” captivating Dante, Virgil, and the other souls in listening.

While Dante, Virgil, and the souls remain absorbed in Casella’s song, the venerable Cato reproaches them for their slowness and orders them to run to the mountain to strip themselves of what prevents God’s manifestation. Like frightened doves that suddenly abandon their food, the souls interrupt the song and flee toward the shore without knowing where they will arrive; Dante and Virgil too depart with equal speed.

## Canto 3

While the souls flee toward the mountain after Cato’s rebuke, Dante clings to Virgil, who appears remorseful for his own delayed slowness. When Dante sees only his own shadow on the ground and fears that he has been abandoned, Virgil explains that his body is buried in Naples and that, being a spirit, he casts no shadow. Virgil states that human reason cannot understand divine mysteries, such as the way souls suffer or the Trinity, and invites men to be content with the limits of their knowledge. Finally, he recalls with distress Aristotle, Plato, and the other pagan sages, condemned eternally to desire without result the knowledge of God.

Dante and Virgil arrive at the foot of the mountain and find a wall so steep that it seems impossible to climb. While Virgil tries to understand from which side the slope can be climbed, Dante catches sight on the left of a group of souls advancing very slowly, and the two head toward it to ask for advice. The souls stop near the bank, timid and gathered like sheep following the first of the flock, and Virgil asks them for the way upward. Seeing Dante’s shadow cast upon the rock, they draw back in amazement; Virgil explains that Dante has a human body and that a heavenly power guides them, and then the souls signal to them to go forward.

One of the souls asks Dante whether he has ever seen him; blond, handsome, and wounded in battle, he reveals himself to be Manfred, grandson of the Empress Constance. Manfred recounts that, after being mortally struck, he repented in tears and entrusted himself to God, whose infinite goodness welcomes whoever turns to it, despite the horror of his sins. He recalls that the pastor of Cosenza, acting on Clement’s order, had his bones exhumed near Benevento and had them carried outside the kingdom, along the Verde. He explains, however, that the Church’s curse cannot prevent a return to eternal love as long as hope remains, although whoever dies in contumacy against the Church must wait outside that bank thirty times the duration of his rebellion, unless the prayers of the living shorten this punishment. He therefore begs Dante to tell his daughter Constance the truth about his fate and this prohibition, because the suffrages of the living can greatly benefit the souls in the afterlife.

## Canto 4

Dante reflects on the fact that, when the soul is completely absorbed by a pleasure or a pain, it does not perceive the passing of time; thus, while he was listening to and admiring Manfred, the sun had risen by fifty degrees without his noticing it. The souls show the two poets the passage they were seeking, a very narrow and harsh fissure in the rock, more difficult than the ascents of Sanleo, Noli, Bismantova, and Cacume. Following Virgil, who gives him hope and light, Dante faces the ascent with the wings of great desire, since the ground requires the use of both feet and hands.

Having reached the upper edge of the high cliff, Dante asks Virgil which road they should take. Virgil orders him to continue climbing behind him, until an experienced guide appears. The summit is very high and the slope so steep that Dante, exhausted, begs his master to stop. Encouraged by Virgil, he reaches with difficulty a nearby ledge, where the two sit facing east, toward the place from which they had climbed. Dante first looks at the shores below, then at the sun, and marvels at being struck by its light from the left.

Virgil, noticing Dante’s astonishment at the position of the sun, explains to him through an astronomical argument that Mount Purgatory and Zion have the same horizon but opposite hemispheres: therefore the equator and the course of the sun appear here turned toward the north, whereas to the Hebrews they appeared toward the warm south. Dante declares that he finally understands what had previously seemed obscure to him and asks how much climbing remains. Virgil replies that the mountain is more strenuous at its base and becomes progressively easier, and that the journey will be finished when climbing proves as easy as taking a ship downstream with the current.

After Virgil’s words, a nearby voice calls them toward a great boulder, in whose shade indolent souls are resting. Dante recognizes Belacqua, who sits wearily embracing his knees, and asks him why he remains there. Belacqua explains that the angel at Purgatory’s gate does not yet allow him to begin his punishments: he must wait outside for a time equal to that in which he delayed good sighs in life, unless prayers from living people in grace shorten the wait. Meanwhile Virgil urges Dante to continue, since the sun has reached noon and night already covers Morocco.

## Canto 5

While Dante follows Virgil, some souls notice with wonder that his shadow interrupts the light and understand that he is alive. Virgil rebukes him because he lets himself be distracted by their comments and urges him to continue, firm like a tower against the wind. Another band of souls, singing the «Miserere», sends two messengers to ask who they are; Virgil confirms that Dante has a body of true flesh and suggests that they honor him. The crowd then rushes toward the two to address prayers to Dante, while Virgil tells him to keep walking and listen to them.

The souls of those who died violently, sinners until their final hour, call to Dante and ask him to stop and carry news of them to earth; enlightened by heaven, they repented and forgave before dying, becoming reconciled with God. Dante, although recognizing no one, promises to help them, and Jacopo del Cassero asks him for prayers in Fano. Jacopo recounts that the lord of Este, who hated him unjustly, had him killed near Padua: attacked at Oriago, he fled toward Mira, but fell entangled in a marsh and died there from loss of blood.

Bonconte da Montefeltro asks Dante to remember him with compassion, since neither Giovanna nor anyone else cares for him. Asked about his disappearance after Campaldino, he recounts that he arrived wounded in the throat near the Archiano, where he lost sight and speech, invoked Mary, and died. An angel took his soul because of a single tear of repentance, while a demon, furious at having lost it, took revenge on his body. The demon raised a violent storm that dragged the corpse into the Arno and covered it with mud and debris.

A third spirit, Pia, asks Dante to remember her when he has returned to the world and rested from the long journey. She says sorrowfully that Siena gave her life and Maremma took it from her, adding that the one who had married her by placing the wedding ring on her finger knows the truth.

## Canto 6

Dante compares himself to the winner of a dice game, besieged by the crowd, and frees himself from the souls by promising to remember them in the world. Among the spirits who died violent deaths, he recognizes the Aretine killed by Ghino di Tacco, another drowned during a pursuit, Federigo Novello, and the Pisan who made good Marzucco appear strong. He also sees Count Orso and Pier da la Broccia, who declares that he was separated from his body through hatred and envy, not for a crime committed, and warns the lady of Brabant, still alive, to take care not to end up in a worse company.

Dante asks Virgil to clarify the apparent contradiction between the statement that prayer cannot bend Heaven’s decree and the souls’ hope of shortening their purification through others’ prayers. Virgil replies that their hope is not vain: divine justice is not diminished, since the fire of love can accomplish at once what the souls must satisfy by remaining in Purgatory. The words cited by Dante instead concerned prayers disconnected from God; for a complete explanation of this lofty doubt, he must await Beatrice, whom he will see happy on the mountain’s summit.

Dante, feeling less weary, urges Virgil to hasten the ascent, since the mountain is already casting its shadow. Virgil replies that they will not be able to reach the summit before the sun sets and points out a solitary soul who will be able to show them the quickest way. The Lombard soul remains proud and silent like a resting lion and, instead of answering Virgil’s request, asks from what land and life they come. When Virgil pronounces the name of Mantua, it reveals itself as Sordello, his fellow countryman, and the two embrace.

Struck by the readiness with which a soul welcomes its fellow citizen at the mere sound of their homeland, Dante bursts into a sorrowful invective against Italy, enslaved, torn by internal wars, and deprived of imperial guidance, like a ship without a pilot and a brothel. He recalls that Justinian gave it laws, but denounces the Church, which should be devout and leave Caesar to temporal rule, for having made Italy unruly by interfering with imperial power. He curses Albert the German and his father for having, held back by greed for German lands, left the garden of the Empire deserted, and invites him to see the conflicts of the Montecchi, the Cappelletti, the Monaldi, and the Filippeschi, the affliction of the nobles, and Rome widowed and alone. Finally, he asks God whether His just eyes have turned elsewhere or whether Italian sufferings belong to an incomprehensible design, since every city is full of tyrants and every partisan commoner becomes a Marcellus.

Dante directs a bitterly sarcastic invective at Florence, pretending to praise it because, unlike war-torn Italy, it would be rich, peaceful, and wise. He derides its citizens, ready to proclaim justice and spontaneously take on public offices, and denounces their political presumption. Compared with ancient Athens and Sparta, Florence produces measures so minute and unstable that those devised in October do not last until mid-November; it continually changes laws, currency, magistracies, and customs. He concludes by comparing the city to a sick woman who, unable to find rest in bed, keeps turning without respite to lessen her own pain.

## Canto 7

After repeated courteous and joyful greetings, Sordello asks the two travelers who they are. Virgil reveals that he was buried by order of Octavian before souls worthy of it could rise to God and that he lost Heaven solely through lack of faith. Amazed, Sordello bows and embraces him, hailing him as the glory of the Latins and asking from which circle of Hell he comes. Virgil replies that he has traveled through all Hell by divine will and explains that he dwells in Limbo, devoid of torments but shrouded in darkness and sighs, together with innocents who died before baptism and the righteous who did not know the theological virtues. Finally, he asks Sordello to indicate the quickest way to the point where Purgatory properly begins.

Sordello explains that they can climb and go around as far as he can guide them, but that, as day declines, it is impossible to proceed upward at night. Drawing a line on the ground, he clarifies that it is not an external obstacle that prevents the ascent, but the nighttime darkness, which hinders the will, while allowing them to descend or walk along the mountainside. He therefore proposes reaching some secluded souls on the right and awaiting the new day in a hollow of the mountain; Virgil agrees, and the three head toward that place.

A slanting path between steep and level ground leads the three along the side of a mountain hollow. The grass and flowers of the valley possess colors that would surpass gold, silver, and every other hue, while a thousand sweet scents blend there indistinctly. The souls, seated on the greenery and among the flowers, sing the “Salve, Regina.” Virgil advises them not to descend among them yet before sunset, since from the ledge they will be able to recognize their gestures and faces better.

From the top of the ledge, Sordello points out Emperor Rudolf, guilty of not healing Italy’s wounds, and Ottokar of Bohemia, superior to his son Wenceslas, devoted to lust and idleness. He then recognizes Philip III of France, who died fleeing and dishonoring the lily, and the father and father-in-law of the “evil of France,” grieved by his vicious life. He also indicates Charles of Anjou and Peter of Aragon, observing that Charles’s worth and that of a young descendant of his did not pass to the other heirs, nor does Peter’s lineage equal its father. Sordello concludes by showing Henry of England, whose descendants are better, and William, marquis of Montferrat, whose death makes Montferrat and Canavese weep, and states that human virtue rarely passes through lineage.

## Canto 8

At sunset, a soul rises among the spirits and devoutly intones the evening hymn “Te lucis ante”; the others follow it, turning their eyes toward the celestial spheres. Dante warns the reader that the allegorical veil is now thin. Then two angels in green garments and with green wings, bearing flaming swords but lacking their points, descend from above and take positions at the sides of the valley. Sordello explains that they are sent from Mary’s bosom to protect it from the serpent that is about to arrive, and Dante, terrified, presses close to Virgil’s trusted shoulders.

Sordello leads Dante and Virgil among the great shades of the valley, where Judge Nino Visconti recognizes Dante and is glad to see him not among the damned. Astonished to learn that he is still alive, Nino and Sordello also call Currado to behold this divine grace. Nino asks Dante to tell his daughter Giovanna to pray for him and bitterly laments that his wife, having laid aside her white widow’s veils and remarried, no longer loves him. He states that the woman’s new family, symbolized by the Milanese viper, will not give her as fine a burial as the rooster of Gallura, emblem of his own family, would have given her.

Dante gazes at the sky and notices three bright stars, while Virgil explains to him that the four clear stars seen in the morning have set and these have risen in their place. Sordello then calls their attention to a serpent, their adversary, which enters the small valley from the unprotected side and moves among the grass and flowers. At the rustling of the green wings of the two celestial angels, the serpent flees and the angels return together to their posts.

A soul who had approached the judge Nino Visconti when he called addresses Dante. He introduces himself as Currado Malaspina, descendant of his namesake ancestor, and asks him for news of the Val di Magra and the nearby lands. Dante replies that he has never been in those places, but celebrates the European fame of the Malaspina house for generosity and valor, stating that it alone remains upright in a corrupt world. Currado predicts to him that, before the sun returns seven times to the constellation of Aries, Dante will have personally experienced the truth of such praise, if the course of judgment is not halted.

## Canto 9

Shortly before dawn, while the dawn appears in the east and the night draws to an end, Dante, overcome by sleep, falls asleep on the grass with the other five. In the hour when dreams are almost prophetic, he seems to see an eagle with golden feathers that snatches him and carries him up to the sphere of fire; the heat of the dreamed fire is so intense that it wakes him.

Dante awakens bewildered and petrified by fear, like Achilles when he was carried away asleep by his mother, and finds Virgil beside him. Virgil reassures him: while he slept, Lucia took him and carried him to Purgatory, showing Virgil the open entrance. Comforted by this explanation, Dante follows his guide up the slope toward the entrance.

Dante warns the reader that he is elevating the subject matter of his account and will therefore support it with greater art. With Virgil, he reaches the gate of Purgatory, accessible by three steps of different colors, guarded by an angel whose face is unbearably radiant and who is armed with a naked sword. Virgil explains that a lady of Heaven directed them to that gate, and the courteous gatekeeper invites them to come forward. Dante describes the steps: the first is of white marble, polished like a mirror; the second is of dark stone, rough, scorched, and cracked; the third is of porphyry flaming like blood. The angel sits on the threshold, like a diamond stone, with both feet upon the highest step.

Virgil leads Dante up the three steps and orders him to humbly ask the guardian to open the gate. Dante throws himself at the angel’s feet, asks for mercy, and strikes his breast three times; the angel inscribes seven P’s upon his forehead with the point of his sword and tells him to wash those wounds inside. Then he draws two keys, one of gold and one of silver, and opens the gate by using first the white one and then the yellow one. He explains that one key is more precious, while the other requires greater art and skill to untie the knot, and that he received them from Peter with the command to prefer erring in opening rather than in keeping shut one who prostrates himself at his feet. After warning that whoever looks back returns outside, he opens the sacred gate, whose hinges resound thunderously; Dante then hears the “Te Deum laudamus” mingled with a sweet sound, with words discernible only at intervals, as in singing accompanied by an organ.

## Canto 10

Having entered through the gate that the evil love of souls makes appear crooked, Dante and Virgil hear it close, and Dante observes that he would have no excuse if he turned to look at it. They then climb a narrow fissure in the rock, winding from one side to the other like a wave according to Virgil’s instructions. The path is so slow that the moon sets before they emerge from that passage; finally, weary and uncertain of their direction, they reach a solitary level place. The first terrace appears about three times the height of a man in width, between the edge of the void and the wall of the mountain rising inward.

On the first terrace Dante recognizes in the inner wall a cliff of white marble, carved by God with images so perfect as to surpass Polyclitus and nature itself. The first depicts the angel of the Annunciation, who seems to say “Ave” to Mary, who answers “Ecce ancilla Dei” with a humble bearing. There follow David, who dances humbly before the holy Ark while his wife Michal looks at him with contempt, and the emperor Trajan, who delays his departure to render justice to a widow whose son has been killed. The sculptures are so lifelike as to engage sight, hearing, and smell and to make the dialogue of Trajan and the widow appear as visible words, offering examples of humility that Dante contemplates with pleasure.

Virgil points out many souls who advance slowly and who will guide them toward the higher terraces, while he warns the reader not to lose heart before the way in which God demands payment of the debt of sin. Dante at first does not recognize them as people, but Virgil explains that the weight of their torment huddles them down to the ground beneath enormous stones. Addressing proud Christians, Dante rebukes them because, blind in mind, they trust in backward steps without understanding that they are worms destined to become angelic butterflies turned toward justice. The penitents, more or less bent according to the weight they carry, resemble carved figures that support a roof with their knees drawn to their chests; even the most patient among them seem to weep: “I can no more.”

## Canto 11

The souls of the proud recite, beneath the weight of their punishments, a paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer, praising God, asking for the peace of his kingdom, daily bread and forgiveness, and requesting to be delivered from the tempter. The final request is not for them, since they have no need of it, but for those who have remained on earth. As they proceed weary and variously afflicted along the first terrace, purging the world's darkness, Dante observes that, if they pray for the living, the living must pray and act to wash away the stains they brought from the world, so that they may rise pure and light to the starry spheres.

Virgil asks the souls of the proud to show Dante, weighed down by his mortal body, the shortest and least steep way to climb to the stairway. A voice invites them to follow along the right-hand edge and reveals itself to be that of Omberto Aldobrandesco, son of Guglielmo and born in Tuscany. Omberto confesses that the ancient nobility of his lineage and the glorious deeds of his ancestors made him so proud that he despised every man; this pride led him to death and to the ruin of his relatives. Forced to carry the boulder that bends his head, he atones among the dead for what he did not repent of while alive, until he has satisfied God.

While he walks bent down with the souls of the proud, Dante is recognized and called by Oderisi of Gubbio, a famous manuscript illuminator. Oderisi admits that Franco Bolognese now shines more than he does in the art of illuminating manuscripts and confesses that he is atoning for the pride born from the desire to excel, since he turned to God before dying. He states that human glory is vain and fleeting, recalling that Giotto has eclipsed Cimabue and that one Guido has taken poetic glory from the other, perhaps awaiting a third who will surpass them both. He compares fame to the wind and to the color of grass, and cites a once celebrated lord of Siena, once known throughout Tuscany, who is now barely whispered of in his city.

Dante says that Oderisi's truthful words inspire humility in him and lessen his pride, then asks him who the man just named is. Oderisi replies that he is Provenzan Salvani, punished because he wished to subject all Siena to his own power. Dante asks how he was able to ascend without serving the waiting time reserved for those who repent only at the end of life, unless aided by good prayers. Oderisi explains that Provenzano, at the height of his glory, publicly humbled himself in the Campo of Siena to collect the money needed to free a friend who was a prisoner of Charles; he adds obscurely that Dante's neighbors will soon make him understand the meaning of that act.

## Canto 12

Dante proceeds alongside the soul bent beneath the weight of its punishment, until Virgil orders him to leave it and continue, since each person must propel his own boat. Dante straightens his body, although he retains humble and chastened thoughts, and follows his master more lightly. Virgil tells him to look at the ground, where examples that make the journey safer are depicted with an art superior to that of images on tombs.

Dante sees on the hard pavement images of punished proud figures: Lucifer struck by lightning from heaven, Briareus and the Giants cast down, Nimrod beside the tower of Shinar, and Niobe, Saul, Arachne, and Rehoboam humbled by their ruin. Also depicted are Almeon killing his mother, Sennacherib's sons leaving him dead in the temple, Tomyris filling Cyrus with blood, the flight of the Assyrians after Holofernes's death, and Troy reduced to ashes. The works appear so perfect that the dead and the living seem real; Dante concludes by sarcastically warning Eve's children not to proceed proudly without lowering their faces to recognize their evil path.

Virgil invites Dante to raise his head and no longer linger, since the day is now far advanced, and points out to him an angel returning from his service. The angel, dressed in white and shining like a trembling morning star, welcomes them near the steps and laments that so few men answer the invitation to ascend, although they were born to fly high. He leads them to the place where the rock is cut, strikes Dante on the forehead with his wing, and promises him a safe passage upward.

Dante and Virgil climb a steep stairway, compared to the one that leads to a church in Florence, and hear «Beati pauperes spiritu» being sung. Dante feels that he has become much lighter and asks Virgil what weight has been removed from him. Virgil explains that one of the seven P's engraved on his forehead has been almost completely erased and that, when the others too have been wiped away, climbing will no longer be labor but pleasure. Dante then touches his forehead and discovers that only six remain, while Virgil smiles.

## Canto 13

Dante and Virgil reach the second terrace of Purgatory, where envy is purged: it is a bare place of livid stone, without shadows or visible signs of presences. Finding no one from whom to ask the way, Virgil relies on the sun to guide them. As they walk, they hear invisible spirits flying and proclaiming examples of love and charity: «Vinum non habent», «I am Orestes», and «Love those from whom you received evil». Virgil explains that these voices are the whip of love that punishes envy and adds that, before the passage to forgiveness, they will hear the curb made up of examples of the opposite vice.

Virgil points out to Dante some souls seated along the rocky wall: they are wrapped in cloaks the color of the stone, support one another, and invoke Mary, Michael, Peter, and all the saints. Dante is deeply moved by them, because they wear rough hair shirts and have their eyelids pierced and sewn with iron wire, as is done to wild sparrowhawks. Since it seems unjust to him to observe them without being seen, he turns to Virgil, who allows him to speak with brevity and wisdom. Dante wishes them swift purification and asks whether there is among them an Italian soul, whom he would be glad to know.

A soul replies that every soul is a citizen of the true city, but that Dante is seeking someone who lived as a pilgrim in Italy; when questioned, she introduces herself as Sapia, a Sienese woman who atones for her wicked life along with the others. Although her name meant wise, she confesses that she rejoiced more in others' misfortunes than in her own good fortune, and recounts having delighted in the defeat of her fellow citizens near Colle, going so far as to defy God. Having repented only at the end of her life, she would still be obliged to do penance if Pier Pettinaio had not remembered her in his holy prayers. Dante declares that his eyes will be closed for only a short time because of envy, but that he fears far more the torment of the terrace below, reserved for pride; he then reveals that he is alive and accompanied by Virgil. Astonished, Sapia asks him to restore her reputation among her relatives in Tuscany and mocks the vain projects of the Sienese at Talamone and in the search for the Diana, which will cost the admirals still more.

## Canto 14

Two spirits, bent toward one another, notice that Dante is traveling around the mountain still in his mortal body and can open and close his eyes; amazed, they gently ask him who he is and where he comes from. Dante describes without naming it a Tuscan river that rises in Falterona and along which he bears his own body, adding that his name is not yet sufficiently known. One of the two recognizes the Arno, while the other wonders why Dante has kept its name silent, as one does with horrible things.

The questioned soul, Guido del Duca, declares that the name of the Arno valley deserves to perish, because its inhabitants flee virtue as an enemy and have degenerated as though Circe had transformed them into beasts. Following the river’s course, he compares them to pigs near its source, to curs, then to wolves, and finally to deceitful foxes. He then predicts to Rinieri da Calboli that his grandson Fulcieri will hunt the Florentine wolves, sell them alive, and kill them cruelly, leaving the city bloodied and devastated; Rinieri grows sorrowful on hearing it.

Eager to know the names of the two souls, Dante asks them, and Guido del Duca reveals himself, identifying the other as Rinieri da Calboli. He confesses that in life he was so consumed by envy that he suffered at others’ joy and that he now reaps the fruit of that sin. He then deplores the moral decline of Romagna, once a land of noble and courteous men and families, but now filled with wicked people and degenerate or extinct lineages. Painfully recalling the worthy people of the past and the disappearance of nobility of spirit, he gives himself over to weeping and dismisses Dante, because the thought of their condition oppresses his soul.

Knowing that Guido del Duca and Rinieri could hear them moving away, Dante and Virgil continue in silence until two terrible voices tear through the air: one cries Cain’s words, the other declares herself Aglauros, transformed into stone. Frightened, Dante draws close to Virgil and moves to his right. When calm returns, Virgil explains that those voices are the harsh bridle intended to keep human beings within the proper limit, but they allow themselves to be drawn by the bait of the ancient adversary and, ignoring the eternal beauties shown by heaven in order to turn their gaze toward the earth, deserve divine punishment.

## Canto 15

As Dante and Virgil walk westward, the sun is now near vespers in Purgatory, while on earth it is midnight. A refracted light, more intense than before, dazzles Dante: Virgil explains to him that it is a heavenly messenger who has come to invite them to ascend and that such visions will soon give him pleasure. Having reached the angel, they are directed to a stairway less steep than the others; as they climb, they hear behind them «Beati misericordes» and «Rejoice, you who overcome».

Climbing alone, Dante asks Virgil to explain to him the words of the Romagnol spirit about the «prohibition» and the «sharer». Virgil clarifies that envy arises from earthly goods, which diminish when they are shared, whereas the infinite good of heaven grows for each person the more souls love and share it, like light reflected among mirrors. Dante, still doubtful about how a distributed good can enrich more, is referred to Beatrice for a full explanation; Virgil also urges him to make the five wounds of sin that remain on his forehead disappear soon.

As he is about to tell Virgil that his explanation satisfies him, Dante reaches the next terrace and is immediately carried away in an ecstatic vision. He sees three examples of meekness: Mary, who gently reproaches the young Jesus in the temple; Pisistratus, who calmly answers his wife, desirous of revenge against the one who embraced their daughter; and Saint Stephen, who, stoned by a furious crowd, prays to God to forgive his persecutors.

When his soul returns from the visions to reality, Dante rouses himself as from sleep, after having walked for a long time with veiled eyes and unsteady legs. Virgil tells him that he knows his thoughts and explains that what he saw was meant to open his heart to the waters of peace that flow from the eternal source. His question did not arise from ignorance, but served to restore strength to Dante's steps, because one must spur on those who are slow to resume wakefulness.

At dusk, Dante and Virgil advance, looking as far as they can against the bright evening rays. A cloud of smoke, black as night and impossible to avoid, gradually approaches them, taking away their sight and the pure air.

## Canto 16

The smoke, denser and more irritating than any infernal darkness, forces Dante to close his eyes and follow Virgil as a blind man follows his guide. In the darkness he hears the souls of the wrathful invoking in unison the Lamb of God for peace and mercy. A voice asks him who he is, since he speaks like a living man; Dante explains his journey and asks for the way. The spirit reveals himself as Marco Lombardo, confirms that they are ascending in the right direction, and asks him to pray for him when he is above.

Dante promises Marco Lombardo to fulfill his request, but asks him to resolve the doubt about the cause of the world's corruption, which some attribute to heaven and others to human beings. Marco, sighing painfully, replies that human beings, blind, attribute every cause to heaven, as though it imposed everything by necessity. Heaven initiates some impulses, but human beings possess the light to distinguish good and evil and free will, subject to a higher power and nature that create the mind; therefore, the cause of the world's straying lies within human beings themselves.

Marco explains that the soul, simple and inclined toward small goods, needs laws and a sovereign to guide its love. The corruption of the world derives from bad leadership: the Church, joining the pastoral care to the sword of temporal power, has extinguished one of the two «suns» that Rome once had to show the paths of God and of the world. He laments the disappearance of virtue and courtesy in the land between the Adige and the Po, where only Currado da Palazzo, the good Gherardo, and Guido da Castel remain. Dante asks about Gherardo; Marco says that he knows no other surname for him except the one derived from his daughter Gaia, and takes his leave when dawn brightens the smoke and the angel awaits him.

## Canto 17

Having emerged from the smoke that had veiled his sight, Dante reflects on the power of imagination, moved by a heavenly light when the senses offer it no material. Carried away in visions that isolate him from the external world, he sees the trace of the cruelty of Procne, transformed into the bird that most loves to sing, then Haman crucified before Ahasuerus, Esther, and Mordecai. Finally Lavinia appears to him, weeping for her mother Amata, who killed herself in anger for fear of losing her daughter.

A light far more intense than earthly light interrupts Dante's visions, and a voice announces that the ascent is there. Virgil explains to him that it is a divine spirit that shows them the way upward without being asked and urges him to climb before darkness falls. Having reached the first step, Dante feels a beating of wings and a breath upon his face, while he hears the beatitude of the peacemakers; as night and the stars arrive, he feels the strength of his legs fail.

Stopped where the stair rose no farther, Dante asks Virgil what fault is purified on the new terrace. Virgil replies that there the love of the good lacking its due ardor, that is, negligence, is restored, and begins an explanation of the nature of love. Every creature possesses natural or rational love: the former never errs, whereas the latter can sin by choosing an evil object or by loving the good with excessive or insufficient vigor; therefore love is the seed of every virtue and of every action worthy of punishment.

Virgil continues by explaining that no one can hate himself or God, on whom every being depends; thus the evil of love must be directed toward one’s neighbor. He distinguishes three forms: those who wish to lower their neighbor in order to excel, those who fear losing power, favor, honor, or fame because of another’s success, and those who, feeling offended, crave revenge; these perverse loves are purified on the lower terraces. He then treats deficient love for the true good, in which every soul seeks repose: those who are slow to seek or attain it are punished here after due repentance. There are also goods that do not give happiness and are not the supreme Good, and excessive love for them is purified on the three higher terraces, whose divisions Virgil leaves Dante to discover.

## Canto 18

Dante asks Virgil to explain love, the principle from which every good work and its opposite derive. Virgil states that the soul, naturally disposed to love, turns toward what the intellect presents to it as pleasing, and that this motion, like fire rising, becomes desire until it reaches the thing loved. Since every love seems to have good matter, many mistakenly believe that it is always praiseworthy; Dante then asks how merit or blame can exist if love necessarily arises from an external object. Virgil explains that the first appetites are natural and deserve neither praise nor blame, but man possesses an innate faculty that judges and guards assent, free will, upon whose use moral responsibility depends; Beatrice will clarify for him what surpasses reason.

Toward midnight, Dante, drowsy after Virgil’s explanations, is awakened by a crowd of souls running along the terrace, urged by good will and just love to expiate sloth. Two of them cry out the examples of Mary’s and Caesar’s promptness, while the others urge them not to waste time through scant love and to revive grace through commitment to good. Virgil asks for the passage upward, and one soul, formerly abbot of San Zeno in Verona, replies while running that they must follow them; he then foretells the ruin of the monastery, entrusted by its true pastor to an unworthy son, sick in body and still more in mind. When the abbot is already far away, Virgil points out to Dante two souls approaching and crying out examples of sloth punished.

The souls at the rear cry out the examples of sloth punished: the people of Israel, dead before seeing the Jordan and its descendants, and those who, not enduring hardship to the end with the son of Anchises, offered themselves to a life without glory. When the shades are by then too far away to be seen, Dante loses himself in many and diverse thoughts, closes his eyes because of sleep, and his thought transforms into a dream.

## Canto 19

Shortly before dawn, Dante dreams of a stammering, cross-eyed, deformed, mutilated, and pale woman. As he looks at her, his gaze loosens her tongue, straightens her, and colors her face, until she becomes a sweet Siren who sings that she led Ulysses astray and fully satisfies whoever grows accustomed to her. A holy woman appears and calls Virgil; he seizes the Siren, opens her garments, and shows her belly, whose stench awakens Dante.

Dante rises after Virgil has called him three times, and the two resume their journey with the sun at their backs. An angel with wings like those of a swan invites them to pass between the rock walls and proclaims blessed those who weep, because they will be consoled. Dante, still absorbed in the vision of the Siren, confesses to Virgil that he cannot turn his thoughts away from her. Virgil reminds him that he has seen the ancient witch and how man frees himself from her, and urges him to look toward the heavenly call of the eternal King; then Dante, like a falcon drawn by food, revives and climbs to the next terrace.

Having reached the fifth terrace, Dante sees souls lying face down on the ground, weeping and sighing the words of the psalm, «Adhaesit pavimento anima mea». Virgil asks them for the quickest way upward, and a voice replies to keep the right side always toward the outside. With Virgil’s consent, Dante approaches the creature that spoke and asks who it was, why the souls lie turned toward the ground, and whether it wishes him to obtain something for it in the world of the living.

The soul reveals that he was a successor of Peter and came from the family of the counts of Lavagna, near Sestri and Chiavari. He recounts that only after becoming shepherd of Rome did he understand how deceptive earthly life was, and that he converted late from avarice to the love of God. He explains that the avaricious penitents lie stretched out and bound with their faces to the ground, because in life they fixed their gaze on earthly goods and smothered love for every good. When Dante kneels in reverence, he orders him to rise, because in the other life they are all companions under a single authority and earthly dignities have no value. He then urges him to continue and mentions his niece Alagia, good by nature if her family’s bad reputation does not corrupt her, as the only relative he has left in the world.

## Canto 20

Dante reluctantly leaves Pope Adrian V and continues with Virgil along the terrace of the avaricious, cursing the ancient she-wolf of avarice and wondering when the one who will drive her away shall come. He then hears a soul who, weeping, praises examples of poverty and generosity: Mary, Fabricius, and Nicholas, who aided maidens in order to save their honor. Drawn by its words, Dante asks it who it is and why it alone renews such praises, promising to repay its account if he returns to complete the short journey of mortal life.

The soul reveals himself as Hugh Capet, progenitor of the French royal dynasty, and accuses himself of being the root of the evil plant that, through his descendants, has brought harm to Christendom. He condemns and prophesies the robberies and violences of his lineage, from the deeds of Charles of Anjou and Charles of Valois to the outrage of Philip the Fair against Pope Boniface VIII at Anagni. Finally, he explains that the avaricious, by day, recall examples of poverty and generosity, while by night they cry out examples of punished avarice, such as Pygmalion, Midas, Achan, Sapphira, Heliodorus, Polymestor, and Crassus.

After moving away from Hugh Capet, Dante and Virgil feel the mountain tremble violently, and Dante is seized by a deadly chill. From every side the souls sing «Gloria in excelsis Deo», and the two poets remain motionless and suspended, like the shepherds who first heard that song, until the trembling and the cry cease. They then resume their journey, watching the shades returned to their customary weeping on the ground. Dante is assailed by the desire to understand what has happened, but he does not dare ask questions because of their haste and proceeds timid and pensive.

## Canto 21

Dante is tormented by the natural desire to know, as he follows Virgil along the obstructed path and feels compassion for the souls' just punishment. A shade reaches them from behind, like the risen Christ appearing to the two disciples on the road, and greets them by wishing them God's peace. Virgil explains to it that Dante, still alive and marked by the angel, is destined for salvation, while he guides him from Hell insofar as he is allowed. Then Virgil asks the new soul why the mountain trembled and all the souls cried out together, satisfying Dante's thirst with hope.

The new soul explains that the mountain of Purgatory, above the gate with three steps, is free from every atmospheric phenomenon and every earthly alteration. It trembles only when a soul feels purified and free to ascend, and then the other souls accompany the event with a cry of praise to God. Purification is attested by the will, since divine justice holds the soul against its will in torment until it is released from its inclination to sin. The soul reveals that it has remained in that punishment for more than five hundred years and has only now felt the freedom to reach a better dwelling, causing the earthquake and the praise of the blessed spirits. Virgil, having understood the reason for the prodigy, asks it who it is and why it has stayed there for so many centuries.

The soul reveals itself as Statius, a celebrated poet who lived in the time of Titus, but who did not yet possess the faith then. Born in Toulouse and drawn to Rome by the sweetness of his poetry, he there earned the laurel crown and composed the Thebaid and the unfinished poem on Achilles. He declares that the sparks of his poetic ardor came from Virgil's Aeneid, his mother and nurse in the art of poetry. Finally, he states that, in order to have lived on earth when Virgil was alive, he would agree to remain in Purgatory for one year beyond what was due.

Virgil silently orders Dante to remain quiet, but he cannot restrain a smile, prompted by Statius's words. The shade notices it and asks him why his face has shown that flash of laughter. Dante, torn between the order to be silent and the request for an explanation, receives from Virgil permission to reveal the truth: his guide is that very Virgil from whom Statius drew the inspiration to sing of men and gods. Statius then tries to embrace the master's feet, but Virgil stops him, reminding him that they are both shades; Statius replies that his love makes him forget their insubstantiality, leading him to treat a shade as a solid body.

## Canto 22

The angel who has turned them toward the sixth terrace erases another P from Dante’s forehead and proclaims blessed those who thirst for justice; the three then climb lightly. Virgil asks Statius how avarice could dwell in a man so wise, and Statius clarifies that he was instead prodigal: a line of the Aeneid made him recognize his excess in spending, a sin purged together with its opposite. Then questioned about his conversion, Statius credits Virgil both for his poetic vocation and for his approach to Christianity, since he interpreted the fourth eclogue as an announcement of the new faith. Although baptized before composing the Thebaid, out of fear he remained for a long time a hidden Christian and feigned paganism; such lukewarmness compelled him to expiate for more than four hundred years on the fourth terrace.

Statius, after Virgil has revealed how highly he speaks of him, asks him where Terence, Caecilius, Plautus, and Varro are, and whether they are damned. Virgil replies that they, Persius, and he himself dwell with Homer in the first circle of Hell, together with many Greek poets and figures drawn from Statius's works. The three poets then resume their journey around the mountain, turning to the right according to custom, while the day advances. Virgil and Statius proceed ahead, and Dante follows them alone, listening to their conversations, which nourish his poetic talent.

The poets' sweet conversations are interrupted by a tree with fragrant, delicious fruits, which narrows downward to prevent anyone from climbing it, while clear water falls from the rock and spreads over the leaves. A voice among the branches announces that they will long for that food and sets forth examples of temperance: Mary at the wedding at Cana, the ancient Roman women, Daniel, the people of the first age, and John the Baptist nourished in the desert by honey and locusts.

## Canto 23

While Dante stares at the green tree, Virgil calls him to continue onward. The two hear souls weeping and singing “Labia mea, Domine”: they are the gluttonous, reduced to skeletons by hunger and thirst, who pass by silently and devoutly. Dante marvels that the mere scent of fruit and water arouses in them such tormenting desire, until a soul recognizes him and cries out in wonder at the grace granted to him.

Dante recognizes his friend Forese Donati by his voice, so emaciated that he is unrecognizable in face. Forese asks him for news of himself and of the two souls accompanying him, while Dante, grieved to see his deformed face, asks what consumes him so. Forese explains that a power of the eternal counsel, descended into the water and the tree left behind them, makes the souls of the gluttonous grow thin. The scent of the fruit and water kindles hunger and thirst in them, renewing the punishment at every circuit; but this is in truth a solace, since the will that leads them to the trees was the same that moved Christ to say joyfully “Eli” on the cross to free humankind with his blood.

Dante asks Forese how he could have ascended so soon, since not even five years have passed since his death and he had delayed in repenting. Forese replies that the devout prayers and unceasing weeping of his virtuous widow Nella have drawn him from the terrace of waiting and freed him from the other circles. He praises Nella’s modesty by contrasting it with the shamelessness of Florentine women, and prophesies that they will soon be punished by heaven for immodestly displaying their breasts.

Forese begs Dante no longer to hide his identity from him, since he and all the souls are watching the place where his living body veils the sun. Dante replies that remembering their past life together is still painful, then explains that Virgil turned him away from that life two days earlier, when the moon was full, and led him through the deep night of the true dead with his mortal body. He adds that Virgil is guiding him up the mountain to Beatrice, where he will have to leave him, and also points out the other shade, whose liberation from Purgatory had shortly before made the whole realm tremble.

## Canto 24

Dante and Forese continue their dialogue swiftly, while the skeletal souls watch them in amazement because Dante is alive. Asked about Piccarda and the notable people among the gluttonous, Forese says that his sister already triumphs in Heaven and points out Bonagiunta of Lucca, Pope Martin IV, who expiates eels from Bolsena and vernaccia wine, Ubaldin da la Pila, Bonifazio, and Messer Marchese; all appear pleased to be named.

Dante turns his attention to Bonagiunta of Lucca and hears him murmur the name Gentucca while the soul undergoes the punishment of justice. Bonagiunta prophesies that a woman named Gentucca, still young, will make Dante find the city of Lucca pleasing, despite its bad reputations. He then recognizes Dante as the author of the new poems that begin with “Ladies who have intelligence of love,” and Dante explains that he faithfully writes down what Love dictates to him inwardly. Bonagiunta thus understands the bond that had kept him, the Notary, and Guittone on this side of the sweet new style, since Dante’s and his companions’ pens closely follow the loving dictator.

The souls of the gluttonous hurriedly resume their journey, while Forese stays behind to speak further with Dante and asks him when he will see him again. Dante replies that his return will not be soon, since the city in which he lived strips itself more each day of goodness and seems headed for ruin. Forese then prophesies that the one who bears the greater blame will be dragged by a beast toward a valley without forgiveness, where he will be struck down and leave his body miserably broken. Then Forese departs to catch up with the other souls, because in Purgatory time is precious, and Dante remains with Virgil and Statius.

Dante, Virgil, and Statius encounter a second tree laden with fruit, beneath which the souls stretch out their hands in vain and pray to obtain what the tree refuses. A voice among the branches warns them not to draw near, since the plant derives from the tree bitten by Eve, and recalls the examples of the Centaurs and the Hebrews rejected by Gideon for their sin of gluttony. After walking in silence, the three are stopped by a shining red figure, who points out to them the way upward. A wind from his wings, fragrant with ambrosia, brushes Dante, while the beatitude of those who desire with moderation and hunger for justice is heard.

## Canto 25

When the suitable hour for ascending arrives, Dante, Virgil, and Statius proceed along a narrow stairway. Dante hesitates to ask how souls can grow thin despite having no need of nourishment, but Virgil invites him to speak. After recalling the examples of Meleager and of the image reflected in a mirror, Virgil entrusts Statius with the complete explanation; Statius humbly agrees to clarify the doubt.

Statius begins to explain the generation of the human being: the father’s perfect blood, transformed into seed, joins in the maternal womb with the woman’s blood, disposed to undergo, and acts there by coagulating and enlivening matter. The active power first forms a vegetative soul, like that of a plant, then a sensitive one capable of moving and feeling, and develops the organs of the fetus. When the brain is perfectly articulated, the first mover, God, infuses into it a new spirit, full of virtue, which absorbs what it finds active and becomes a single soul, living, sentient, and capable of reflecting upon itself. To make this process seem less marvelous, Statius compares it to the heat of the sun which, joining with the moisture distilled from the vine, transforms the juice into wine.

When Lachesis exhausts the thread of life, the soul loosens from the flesh, carrying with it the human and divine faculties; the other powers fall silent, while memory, intellect, and will become keener. Without stopping, it falls miraculously upon one of the two shores and, as soon as the place encloses it, its formative power radiates around it into the nearby air. As a reflected ray colors moist air, this power impresses the soul’s form upon the air, creating the shade that follows it as a flame follows fire. The shade organizes all the senses up to sight and, according to the desires and other affections that move it, assumes forms that allow souls to speak, laugh, weep, and sigh: this is the cause of what Dante observes.

Dante and Virgil reach the final punishment, where a flame stretches out from the wall and an upward wind drives it back, forcing them to proceed one at a time along the free side, between fear of the fire and fear of falling. The souls pass through the flames singing the hymn «Summae Deus clementiae» and proclaiming examples of chastity: the Virgin Mary, Diana who drove out Helice, and chaste women and husbands. Such songs and invocations constitute the necessary treatment so that, while the fire purifies them, their final wound of sin may at last be healed.

## Canto 26

Proceeding one behind the other along the edge, Dante casts his shadow upon the flame, making it appear more burning and drawing the attention of the souls. One of them, burned by thirst and fire, asks him how he can make a screen against the sun, as though he had not yet entered the net of death. Before he answers, another band of souls arrives from the opposite direction, and the two multitudes briefly and joyfully kiss one another, like ants that touch antennae to seek their path and their fortune.

After separating from their friendly greeting, the two groups of souls cry out examples of their sins—Sodom and Gomorrah and Pasiphaë—and depart in opposite directions like cranes, returning in tears to their penitential songs. The souls who had questioned Dante draw near to him again, intent on listening to him. He explains that he is still alive in his body and is ascending guided by the grace of a heavenly woman, then asks, while wishing them heaven, who they are and what the other band is. At first the shades remain silent with amazement, like a rough mountaineer before a city, then they quickly recover from their wonder.

The soul who questioned Dante explains that the other band expiates the sin against nature, for which Caesar contemptuously called his opponents «queen», and therefore cries «Sodom»; his own band, instead, sinned through heterosexual lust by bestially following appetite, and recalls Pasiphaë in shame. He then reveals himself to be Guido Guinizzelli, who is purging himself because he repented before dying. Dante, moved to recognize the father of himself and of the poets who composed sweet and graceful love verses, contemplates him for a long time without being able to cross the fire and finally devotedly offers him his service.

Guido Guinizzelli, struck by Dante’s esteem, asks him why he shows him such affection; Dante replies that his sweet verses will remain loved as long as modern usage endures. Guinizzelli points to Arnaut Daniel as the better craftsman of the mother tongue, superior in love verses and romance prose, condemns popular judgments that put fame before truth, and asks Dante for a prayer before disappearing into the fire. Dante approaches Arnaut, who in Provençal says that he weeps for his past folly and joyfully sees the salvation he hopes for, then begs him to remember his suffering in due time and hides himself in the purifying flames.

## Canto 27

At sunset, the angel of God, beside the flame on the bank, sings «Beati mundo corde» and orders the souls to enter the fire in order to proceed. Dante, terrified and imagining human bodies being burned, remains motionless although Virgil assures him that there will be torment but not death and that the flames will not harm even a hair on his head. Only when Virgil reveals to him that this wall separates him from Beatrice does Dante melt from his obstinacy and turn toward his guide. Virgil smiles, enters the fire first, and invites Statius to follow behind them.

Dante passes through the fire, so burning that he would have thrown himself into boiling glass to cool himself, while Virgil comforts him by speaking of Beatrice. A voice guides them out of the flames toward the ascent and, from a light, the invitation «Venite, benedicti Patris mei» resounds; since the sun is setting, they must hurry before darkness falls. When night prevents them from climbing farther, Dante, Virgil, and Statius lie down on a step, like goats watched over by shepherds. Dante contemplates the stars, clearer and larger than usual, until he falls asleep in a sleep capable of foretelling events.

At dawn, Dante dreams of a young and beautiful woman who gathers flowers in a meadow while singing. She says that her name is Leah and that she weaves a garland to adorn herself in the mirror, while her sister Rachel spends the day contemplating her own image: one is fulfilled by acting, the other by seeing. With the first light of dawn, the darkness and the dream flee, and Dante rises to find Virgil and Statius already awake.

Virgil announces to Dante that the sweet fruit sought by the care of mortals will satisfy his hunger that day, arousing in him such an ardent desire to climb that he feels wings growing at every step. Having reached the highest step, Virgil tells him that he has seen the temporal fire and the eternal one and that his reason can discern no farther. After leading him there with intellect and art, he invites him now to take his own pleasure as his guide, among the sun, grass, flowers, and shrubs that the earth produces of itself, while awaiting the beautiful eyes that will come to him weeping. Finally, he declares his will free, upright, and sound, and crowns and miters him over himself.

## Canto 28

Desirous of exploring the divine forest, dense and alive, Dante leaves the bank and advances slowly over the fragrant ground. A sweet, constant breeze brushes his forehead, makes the leaves tremble and bend without interrupting the joyful song of the birds, accompanied by the murmur of the foliage as in the pine forest of Chiassi when Aeolus releases the sirocco. His steps carry him so far into the ancient wood that he can no longer see the point where he had entered.

Dante's path is halted by a clear stream flowing to the left, so pure that it makes all other waters seem impure, although it appears dark beneath the perpetual shadow of the forest. On the other bank he sees a lone woman who sings and gathers flowers, adorning her path with colors. Dante invites her to come closer, saying that she reminds him of Proserpina when she lost her mother and spring; the woman turns modestly and approaches the bank, so that he may hear her song. Although the river separates them by only three steps, Dante feels toward it an aversion greater than Leander's for the Hellespont between Sestos and Abydos.

The woman realizes that Dante and Virgil, new to that place chosen as humanity's dwelling, perhaps marvel at her laughter and feel suspicion because of it. She says that the psalm “Delectasti” can enlighten their intellect and invites Dante, who addressed a request to her, to put to her any other question. Dante replies that the water and the sound of the forest make him doubt a new belief, since they seem to contradict what he had heard previously.

Matelda explains that God created man for goodness and assigned him this place as a pledge of eternal peace, but human fault transformed it from a dwelling of joy into a place of suffering. The mountain rises above the disturbances produced by the exhalations of earth and water, while the motion of heaven makes the forest resound and spreads through the air a generative power that produces different plants on earth. The water of the place springs from a divine source and divides into Lethe, which erases the memory of sin, and Eunoë, which restores the memory of every good deed accomplished. As a further gift, Matelda says that the ancient poets perhaps dreamed here the Golden Age: this was the place of human innocence, perpetual spring, and nectar. Dante then turns to Virgil and Statius, who smile after listening, and then looks back at the woman.

## Canto 29

Matelda concludes her words by singing a verse from the Psalms and sets out along the riverbank, followed by Dante. When the banks turn toward the east, she turns to him and tells him to look and listen. In the great forest, a persistent and ever brighter light suddenly appears, accompanied by a sweet melody. Dante inwardly reproaches Eve's boldness, whose disobedience deprived humanity of the delights of the Earthly Paradise. The air beneath the green branches before them takes on the appearance of a burning fire, while the melody is now distinguishable as songs.

Dante invokes the sacred Virgins, Helicon, and Urania so that they may help him set the magnificent vision into verse. What from afar had seemed to him seven golden trees, as he draws near reveals itself to be seven flaming candelabra, accompanied by the singing of «Hosanna», and Virgil shares his astonishment. Matelda rebukes him because he admires only the living lights without looking at what follows them, and Dante then sees people dressed in white. The little flames advance, leaving seven colored bands in the air, like a rainbow, stretching behind them like banners.

Beneath the seven colored bands, twenty-four elders crowned with lilies advance two by two, singing a blessing addressed to a woman blessed among the daughters of Adam. When they have left the flower-covered ground before Dante free, four animals follow them, each crowned with green foliage, with six feathered wings covered in eyes. Dante refers the reader to Ezekiel's description, specifying, however, that for the number of wings he follows John.

At the center of the procession advances a triumphal chariot on two wheels, drawn by a griffin with golden limbs and white and crimson wings, more splendid than any Roman chariot or that of the Sun. To its right three women dance, red, green, and white, while on its left four women dressed in purple follow a companion with three eyes. Two old men of differing appearance follow, one connected with medicine and the other armed with a sword, then four humble figures and finally a solitary old man who advances sleeping; all wear crowns of roses and red flowers. When the chariot comes before Dante, a thunderclap sounds and the procession stops.

## Canto 30

The septentrion of the first heaven stops, and the procession turns toward the chariot; an elder sings "Veni, sponsa, de Libano" three times, followed by the others. A hundred angels rise upon the chariot, singing "Benedictus qui venis" and scattering flowers. Within a cloud of flowers appears a woman veiled in white, crowned with olive, wrapped in a green mantle, and dressed in flaming red. Although he does not recognize her eyes, Dante perceives her hidden virtue and, trembling, feels the power of his former love.

Struck by the virtue of the veiled woman and recognizing the signs of the ancient flame, Dante turns fearfully toward Virgil, but discovers that his most sweet father and guide has left him. Although weeping over Virgil's disappearance, he hears Beatrice address him by name and tell him not yet to weep for Virgil, since he will have to weep for another sword. The veiled woman, looking at him with severe regality, reveals herself: “I am Beatrice,” and asks him how he dared to ascend the mountain, where the happy man dwells. Overcome by shame, Dante lowers his eyes, while Beatrice's harshness seems to him like a mother's bitter pity toward her son.

The angels sing the psalm “In te, Domine, speravi,” and their compassion melts the ice that gripped Dante’s heart, making him burst into tears and sighs. Beatrice then addresses the heavenly presences and explains that her rebuke must be understood by Dante, so that guilt and sorrow may be of equal measure. She recalls that he had been endowed with exceptional dispositions by divine grace and that, while she was alive, her youthful face kept him turned toward the straight path. After her death, although her beauty and spiritual virtue had increased, Dante moved away from her to follow false images of good, without heeding the inspirations by which she tried to call him back. He fell so low that, in order to save him, nothing remained but to show him the damned people; therefore Beatrice descended to the gate of the dead and obtained the help of the one who led him there. She concludes that the divine order would be violated if Dante crossed Lethe and tasted its water without paying the price of a repentance poured out in tears.

## Canto 31

Beatrice, addressing Dante from the other side of the sacred river, commands him to confess openly the truth of the accusations made against him. Overcome by shame and fear, he manages to utter only a faint “yes,” then dissolves into tears and sighs. Beatrice asks him what worldly obstacles or allurements turned him away from the supreme good and led him to pursue other goods. Weeping, Dante confesses that, as soon as her face was hidden from him by death, present things led him astray with their false pleasure.

Beatrice states that Dante’s confession does not make his guilt less known to the divine judge, but that the spontaneous accusation of sin mitigates, in their court, the severity of punishment. To increase his shame and make him stronger against future seductions, she reminds him that no natural or artistic pleasure surpassed the beauty of her body, now scattered in the earth: if her death deprived him of the highest earthly pleasure, no mortal good should have attracted him. He should have risen up after her instead of allowing himself to be weighed down by deceitful things, like a young bird that easily falls into nets and snares. Humiliated and repentant, Dante laboriously raises his face at her command, sees Beatrice, veiled and beyond the river, turned toward the Griffin and more beautiful than in life, and falls unconscious under the weight of shame and remorse.

Dante, having regained his strength, sees Matelda above him inviting him to hold onto her, and she draws him into the river Lethe up to his throat, walking lightly upon the water. To the sweet sound of the «Asperges me», she immerses him so that he may drink the water and then leads him, wet, into the dance of the four beautiful women. They declare themselves nymphs on earth and stars in heaven, servants of Beatrice from before her descent into the world, and promise to lead him to her eyes, while the three women on the other side will make his vision of their deep meaning keener. Singing, they bring him near the breast of the Griffin, where Beatrice is turned toward them, and urge him not to spare his gaze before her eyes like emeralds, from which Love once hurled his weapons at him.

Dante fixes his gaze on Beatrice's shining eyes, in which the Griffin, while remaining motionless in itself, is reflected as alternately changing its two natures. While his soul, amazed and joyful, feeds on that vision which both satisfies and increases desire, the three theological virtues advance dancing and ask Beatrice to show her faithful one her mouth, her second beauty. The poet finally declares that no one, not even one inspired by Parnassus, could portray Beatrice's splendor when she reveals herself in the open sky.

## Canto 32

Dante keeps his eyes so fixed on Beatrice's holy smile that he no longer perceives his other senses, until the theological virtues warn him that he is looking too intensely. Dazzled for a while by that light, he regains his sight and sees the glorious procession turn, with the chariot, the Griffin, the sun, and the seven flames, to go back. Dante, Matelda, and Statius follow the chariot through the deserted forest to the sound of an angelic melody; after a short walk, Beatrice descends.

Everyone murmurs “Adam” and surrounds the stripped tree, praising the Griffin because he does not break with his beak the sweet wood that is the origin of evil. The Griffin declares that in this way he preserves the seed of all justice, then ties the chariot’s pole to the trunk of the widowed plant. The tree, formerly bare, miraculously renews itself with a color between rosy and violet. While the others sing a hymn that Dante does not understand and cannot recount, he falls asleep like Argus at the sound of the story of Syrinx, and he awakens because of a brightness and a voice that cries out for him to rise.

Dante awakens, like Peter, John, and James after the Transfiguration, and sees Matelda watching over him. In response to his question, Matelda shows him Beatrice, sitting alone beneath the tree’s new foliage and left to guard the chariot tied to the Griffin, while the others in the company ascend to Heaven. The seven nymphs surround her with their lights. Beatrice tells Dante that he will remain only a short time in the Earthly Paradise, but will be with her forever as a citizen of Heavenly Rome, and orders him to watch the chariot and report in writing to the world what he will see. Dante obediently turns his mind and eyes toward the chariot.

Dante witnesses a series of allegorical assaults on the chariot. An eagle plunges upon it, breaking its bark, flowers, and leaves; a fox, seemingly hungry for every good food, rushes into it but is put to flight by Beatrice. The eagle then returns to leave its own feathers in the chariot, while a lament rises from Heaven for the little vessel so badly laden. A dragon then emerges from the earth, drives its tail into the chariot, and tears part of it away; what remains covers itself with the eagle’s feathers, and the sacred structure transforms into a monster with seven heads, three on the pole and one at each corner. Upon the monster sits a prostitute with eyes ready to wander, beside a giant who sometimes kisses her; when she turns her lustful gaze toward Dante, the giant whips her and drags both her and the new beast into the forest.

## Canto 33

The seven women begin to sing in tears, alternating in groups of three or four in the sweet psalm «Deus, venerunt gentes». Beatrice, sighing and compassionate, listens to them almost transformed into Mary at the cross; then, when the other virgins yield speech to her, she rises and tells her beloved sisters that for a little while they will not see her and shortly afterward they will see her again. She then leads the seven women and signals Dante, Matelda, and Statius to follow her; soon afterward she calls Dante closer and asks him why he does not now dare to question her. Overwhelmed by reverence, Dante can barely reply that she already knows his need and what is good for him.

Beatrice orders Dante finally to free himself from fear and shame and reveals to him that the vessel broken by the serpent, once existing and now no longer so, will be avenged by God. She prophesies that the eagle that left its feathers on the chariot will not remain forever without an heir and that an envoy of God, indicated by the enigma «five hundred ten and five», will kill the harlot and her complicit giant. She admits that the prophecy is obscure like the riddles of Themis and the Sphinx, but declares that events will clarify it without harm to flocks or crops. She orders Dante to write down for the living what he has seen, above all the tree twice plundered, and explains that whoever offends it sins against God, since it was created holy for His use alone; the first soul desired for more than five thousand years the one who punished in himself the bite given to that tree. Dante declares that her words are impressed upon his mind like a seal in wax, but asks why their meaning escapes him the more he tries to understand it. Beatrice replies that this shows him the distance between the human school he has followed and the divine way; when Dante states that he does not remember ever having strayed from her, she reminds him of the water of Lethe drunk that day, whose oblivion proves his guilt, and promises henceforth to speak more clearly to his still dull mind.

When the procession stops beside a pale shade, Dante sees the Euphrates and the Tigris emerge from a single spring and slowly separate. He asks Beatrice what this water is that rises from one source and moves away from itself; she refers the explanation to Matelda, who recalls having already told him and attributes his forgetfulness to the water of Lethe. Beatrice adds that a more intense care has darkened his mind and orders Matelda to lead him to Eunoë, so that it may revive his numbed virtue. Matelda leads Dante, accompanied by Statius, to the sacred water; after drinking from it, he returns pure and renewed like a plant putting forth new leaves, ready to rise to the stars.
